# O Level Dynamics

## Apply the chapter conventions

- Use \(g=10\ \mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\) when calculating weight and
  \(g=10\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) when treating gravity as an acceleration.
- Do not silently replace the chapter value with \(9.8\).
- Use a different value only when the question explicitly supplies it.
- Keep reasoning within O Level scope. Use algebra and vector directions, not calculus.
- Cover the syllabus scope:
  - balanced and unbalanced forces, including Newton's first and second laws;
  - free-body diagrams and resultant force;
  - friction;
  - interaction forces and Newton's third law.

## Distinguish mass, weight, and gravitational field strength

- Treat mass as a scalar measure of inertia, measured in kilograms. It does not
  change merely because the object moves to another location.
- Treat weight as the gravitational force on an object, measured in newtons and
  directed along the gravitational field.
- Use

\[
W=mg
\]

  with \(g\) in \(\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\).
- State that gravitational field strength is gravitational force per unit mass.
- When discussing a scale reading or apparent weight, identify the force actually
  measured, usually the normal contact force, rather than automatically calling it
  \(mg\).

## Analyse forces systematically

1. Choose the object or system being analysed.
2. List only the external forces acting **on** that object or system.
3. Draw or describe a free-body diagram with labelled arrows and clear directions.
4. Choose a positive direction and combine forces with signs to find the resultant.
5. Apply

\[
F_{\text{resultant}}=ma.
\]

6. Give the acceleration in the direction of the resultant force and include units.

Use appropriate force names: weight, normal contact force, tension, friction,
air resistance or drag, thrust, lift, and upthrust. Do not invent a "force of
motion".

## Interpret balanced and unbalanced forces

- If \(F_{\text{resultant}}=0\), then \(a=0\). The object is either at rest or
  moving with constant velocity in a straight line.
- Do not claim that balanced forces mean the object must be stationary.
- If \(F_{\text{resultant}}\ne0\), the object accelerates in the direction of the
  resultant force.
- Separate velocity from acceleration: an object can move forward while
  accelerating backward and slowing down.
- Use Newton's first law to explain persistence of rest or uniform straight-line
  motion, and relate greater mass to greater inertia.

## Draw free-body diagrams correctly

- Isolate one named object unless the question explicitly asks for a whole system.
- Show every relevant external force acting on it and no forces that it exerts on
  other objects.
- Start every force arrow on the represented object.
- Label each arrow with a force name or an unambiguous symbol.
- Draw arrow lengths proportionally only when the question says the diagram is to
  scale; otherwise do not infer magnitudes from their lengths.
- For a horizontal surface, include weight and normal contact force unless the
  question tells the student to omit vertical forces.
- Do not add a normal contact force when there is no contact surface.

## Solve connected-body problems

- State any modelling assumption supplied by the question:
  - a smooth surface is frictionless;
  - a taut, light, inextensible string makes connected bodies share the same
    acceleration magnitude;
  - a light string over a light, frictionless pulley has uniform tension.
- Do not assume those conditions when they are not stated or clearly implied.
- First treat all connected bodies as one system to find the common acceleration
  from the external resultant force and total mass.
- Then isolate one body and apply \(F_{\text{resultant}}=ma\) to find tension or a
  contact force.
- For touching blocks, distinguish the force of A on B from the force of B on A.

## Handle Newton's third law

Identify an interaction pair only when the two forces:

- arise from the same interaction;
- are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction;
- are the same type of force;
- act simultaneously on two different objects.

Name both bodies explicitly, for example: "force of the ground on the foot" and
"force of the foot on the ground". Do not call weight and normal contact force a
third-law pair: both act on the same object and may instead be balanced forces.
Third-law forces do not cancel each other in a free-body diagram because they act
on different objects.

## Explain friction, drag, and terminal velocity

- State that friction opposes or tends to oppose relative motion between surfaces
  in contact.
- Do not assume friction always equals the applied force; its value depends on the
  motion and force balance described.
- Treat air resistance as a drag force opposite the motion through air.
- For a falling object at fixed mass and location, keep weight constant while drag
  changes with speed:
  1. initially, weight exceeds drag, so the object accelerates downward;
  2. as speed rises, drag rises and the downward acceleration decreases;
  3. at terminal velocity, drag equals weight, resultant force and acceleration
     are zero, and downward velocity is constant;
  4. after a parachute opens, drag may exceed weight, so the object accelerates
     upward while still moving downward and therefore slows down.

## Generate and mark questions

- Keep generated questions within the stated Dynamics scope and use
  \(g=10\) consistently in the question, solution, and mark scheme.
- Prefer authentic contexts such as vehicles, lifts, parachutists, rockets,
  connected blocks, hanging masses, and objects on rough or smooth surfaces.
- For diagram questions, provide an exact SVG production prompt describing the
  bodies, geometry, force arrows, labels, dimensions, and visual hierarchy. Do not
  reveal the answer through unintended arrow lengths or labels.
- Make all required quantities obtainable from the stem or diagram.
- For multiple-choice questions, use misconception-based distractors without
  introducing a second defensible answer.
- For structured questions, award marks to independent physics steps: correct
  force identification, resultant-force equation, substitution, answer with unit,
  and explanation where required.
- Accept equivalent correct wording when marking conceptual answers.

## Structure worked answers

1. Identify the object or system and state the positive direction.
2. List the forces acting on it.
3. Calculate weight with \(g=10\) when required.
4. Form the signed resultant-force equation.
5. Apply \(F_{\text{resultant}}=ma\).
6. Give the numerical result with units and direction.
7. For explanations, connect force balance to acceleration and then to the change,
   or lack of change, in velocity.
